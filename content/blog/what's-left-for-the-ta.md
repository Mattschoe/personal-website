With the rise of AI in academia, both professors and teaching assistants (TAs) have been put in a hard spot. Taking a course at university has never been more isolated. Where there were once sprawling forums of people forming study groups or asking for help in the middle of the night, there is now silence. Instead, we have students who would rather struggle in private than be the first to ask for a hand.

TAs have seen this more than anyone. Exercise classes and drop-in hours used to be where you went for help, a place to meet someone just a bit more experienced than you and be honest about what you were struggling with. Today they are mostly ghost towns, with only a few regulars showing up. The TA used to be a beacon of knowledge. You brought whatever problem the course had thrown at you, and a really smart person would pull you up when you were drowning. In a sense, the TA still is exactly that. At ITU, the TAs I work with are still some of the smartest, most academically decorated students I know. The problem is that the job itself has changed. Now we have AI, which is there at three in the morning. It never sighs, never makes you feel stupid for asking the same thing twice, and never has a queue. For a student who is already nervous about asking, it removes every reason to raise your hand in front of other people. Help has never been easier to get, and it has never been more private and isolated.

We used to forgive a TA for being hard to understand, because they were so smart. For me that excuse is gone. AI has automated away most of the academic side of the job, and what we are left with is the human side. As a TA at ITU myself, I'll go as far as to admit that a TA doesn't provide much direct academic help anymore. It is no longer the TA who explains how to write a direct proof or how to code a for loop in Java. That role has, or will be, inevitably taken over by AI. But most TAs might not feel this yet, because most of us still teach, explain and guide the way we always have. That is a case of survivorship bias. The students who still show up are the ones the old model still works for, so of course it looks like it works. The ones it stopped working for didn't complain. They just stopped coming. It's no secret among TAs that attendance drops year after year. Students are learning to use AI to understand the material, so they no longer see a reason to show up to exercise classes. So what do we do as TAs? How do we adapt to a generation of students who are more isolated from each other than ever?

I think it's time we start valuing the human side of the TA role much more than the academic side. TAs should focus on helping students study, helping them understand their role and life at a university, and being there when university gets hard. Because AI won't make university easier. If anything, it'll raise the bar for the work that's expected.

That's why I think the most useful thing a TA can teach now is *how to study*, not just *what to study*. The abstraction has gone up a notch. TA'ing is no longer "here's how you solve a direct proof," but "here's how you learn to solve it." AI can be a tool that makes you better, or a crutch you can't walk without once it's taken away. Which one it becomes isn't decided by the tool. It's decided by how you use it, and almost nobody teaches students that. A TA is in the perfect position to, not by banning AI from exercise classes, but by showing what it looks like to use it without leaning on it. At ITU right now, AI has also become a strange taboo. Everyone pretends not to use AI out of fear of being judged, while of course we all do. So for me, future TAs should help destigmatize the tool. They should help students understand the new world they live in and teach them how to use the tools that now belong to every software developer.

Breaking a taboo like that takes honesty, and that asks something much harder of us as TAs. We have to stop being a wall of knowledge, or at least stop making it our priority, and start being a support person for the students. When the TA was the source of answers, it made sense for the TA to be the one who knew everything. Now the answers are everywhere, and what students actually lack is someone to be honest with. They need a TA who can talk openly about their own struggles: feeling behind, not understanding something they "should" understand by now, feeling like everyone else has it figured out. A top-of-the-class TA might be the inspiration a student is looking for, but they'll never be the one who pulls someone from failing to barely passing.

That doesn't mean I'm advocating for academically "bad" TAs. I simply think the human side of the job, like communication, confidence and knowing how to teach, should count for more than a grade in the course. I'd also argue for more diversity in who becomes a TA. A student should be able to see themselves in at least one of their TAs, whether they're failing the course and just need whatever tips and tricks will get them through, or they're at the top and want to stay there. For me, AI changes what a *good* TA looks like. It's less about being impressive and more about being approachable. It's saying "I don't know, let's figure it out" instead of having a perfect answer ready. AI never shows you what it looks like to be stuck and work your way out of it. That has to be the TA's role, and since getting unstuck is where learning happens, that is where we as future TAs can show our value in an AI world.

But all of that only helps the students who actually show up, and fewer and fewer do. That's why I think the most important part of the job is being the middleman in students' human struggles. If students won't come to each other, the TA has to bring them together. That means teaching people how to ask questions out loud and in front of others, without it feeling like a defeat. It means pointing a student toward another student who struggled with the exact same thing last week. And it means deliberately forcing interaction in exercise classes: pairing people up, making them explain things to each other, and making the room something more than twenty people silently prompting their laptops side by side, because asking each other or the TA has become taboo.

One could argue that this has nothing to do with a TA, who is hired to help teach the course material. I think it's the opposite. There's no 15 ECTS course at university called "here's how you learn and study." It's a skill each student picks up along the way, which means every course, and thereby every TA, has an obligation to help students improve it. Learning to ask for help, explain your thinking and work with other people is exactly what the rest of their studies, and their careers, will demand. This might be the most uncomfortable part of the future TA's job, but I truly believe that in a world that's becoming more isolated, with young people interacting less and less, we as TAs have to help where we can and keep human interaction alive and well at university. An AI can explain recursion to you, but it can't introduce you to the person you'll end up studying with for the next three years. You, as a TA, can.

## So what's left?
The TA isn't going away. But the version of the job most of us signed up for, the smart person with the answers, is already gone, or soon will be. What's left is the part AI can't do, and I believe that will be getting students to learn *with each other* instead of alone with a chatbot. The classes are smaller than they used to be. We can keep teaching the few who still show up the way we always have, or we can start figuring out how to bring the rest back.&#32;
